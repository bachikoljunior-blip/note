## C359 — controlled natural language is a qualitatively different route: make the *source itself* a formal language

Attempto Controlled English (ACE) is not arbitrary English with an LLM translator layered on top. It is a restricted English-like language defined by construction/interpretation rules whose sentences have unambiguous formal semantics; the Attempto tooling translates ACE to discourse-representation / first-order-logic forms. In the ACE literature, the key framing is that ACE is effectively a logic language with an English syntax.

Sources:
- https://nlp.stanford.edu/nlaspAbs/fuchs.shtml
- https://arxiv.org/abs/cmp-lg/9603003
- https://aclanthology.org/2021.cnl-1.8/

This changes the semantic-lowering problem. If an authority-bearing policy is authored directly in a controlled language whose semantics is part of the language definition, the grant-critical source is no longer an unconstrained natural-language string. The remaining trusted-computing-base questions become parser/compiler correctness, domain-symbol grounding, and whether the human author actually intended the controlled statement—not whether an LLM silently invented a formal interpretation.

**Scope guard:** ACE does not make arbitrary English unambiguous, and the cited work does not establish a modern machine-checked proof that every implementation of the ACE parser/compiler is correct. The gain is that source semantics are defined by the controlled language rather than inferred ad hoc by a learned translator.

## C360 — controlled-language policy interfaces have already been connected directly to logic-based policy engines

`Modular natural language interfaces to logic-based policy frameworks` (Computer Standards & Interfaces, 2013) maps Attempto Controlled English policy sentences into predicates of many-sorted first-order logic for direct import into logic-based policy-management frameworks. The work emphasizes compositional formal semantics and domain-lexicon generation from logical signatures.

Source:
- https://doi.org/10.1016/j.csi.2013.02.002

This is useful historical evidence that a controlled source language is not merely a theorem-proving curiosity; it has been used as a policy-authoring interface. For the current design, it suggests a strong two-tier authority model:

1. **high-assurance path:** authority-increasing policy changes must be expressed/confirmed in a controlled, formally interpreted source language or another source artifact with machine-checkable semantics;
2. **convenience path:** arbitrary natural language may be translated and validated by learned/roundtrip/static checks, but the result is provisional and can only attenuate authority or request trusted confirmation before an authority increase.

This is strictly stronger than trying to make the arbitrary-NL translator itself the root of authority.

## Refined `SemanticLoweringCertificateV0` activation rule

Add a typed source-semantics field rather than treating all policy sources alike:

```text
source_semantics_class =
  machine_formal
  | controlled_language_formal
  | arbitrary_nl_untrusted
```

Suggested activation rule:

- `machine_formal`: authority delta may be considered under ordinary proof/validation and commit-time witness rules.
- `controlled_language_formal`: authority delta may be considered only if parser/compiler identity/version, domain-symbol mapping and source text are bound into the certificate.
- `arbitrary_nl_untrusted`: automatic validators may reject/attenuate or request repair, but cannot by themselves authorize a positive authority delta. A trusted confirmation/translation witness must bind the candidate formal meaning before activation.

New deterministic regression:

`arbitrary_nl_agreement_cannot_escalate_to_formal_authority` — even if multiple learned translators, roundtrip validators and semantic judges agree, an `arbitrary_nl_untrusted` source cannot move to a more permissive active policy without a trusted source-semantics/confirmation witness.
