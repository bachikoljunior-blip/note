### FE-119 — Shared-role self-play can leak the base model's refusal tendency into the attacker, reducing adversarial pressure in a model-family-specific and checkpoint-stochastic way
**Status:** arXiv v4 preprint, July 2026; direct appendix analysis from Self-RedTeam.

Primary source:
- Liu et al., *Chasing Moving Targets with Online Self-Play Reinforcement Learning for Safer Language Models*, arXiv:2506.07468v4.

Evidence:
- The authors report a Llama3.1-8B-Instruct Self-RedTeam checkpoint where the attacker began refusing attack-revision requests around step 200, while another checkpoint using the same prompt-sampler random seed did not; the checkpoint-level event occurred with empirical probability `<10%` across their runs.
- In a larger audit of 3,000–5,000 revised attacks per model family across checkpoints from iterations 100–200, Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct had **25.27% attacker refusal (1144/4527)**, versus **0.10% / 0.04% / 0.11%** for Qwen2.5 3B/7B/14B.
- Applying the same method to an abliterated Llama variant reduced refusal to **7.21% (339/4705)**, supporting the authors' interpretation that the effect is partly inherited from the base model rather than an unavoidable property of self-play.

Failure implication: if the same safety-aligned policy instantiates both attacker and defender, the attacker can inherit the defender's refusal behavior and silently reduce red-team pressure. Attacker refusal rate therefore needs explicit monitoring; apparent defender improvement can coexist with a weaker endogenous adversary.

Scope guard: this is not a clean single-factor parameter-sharing ablation. The elevated refusal is strongly Llama-specific in the reported audit, Qwen does not show it, and the method still improves downstream defender safety in the authors' evaluation. It does not establish that shared-parameter self-play is generally inferior.

### FE-120 — Supervised activation steering can look strong on seen jailbreak families while retaining roughly 29–32% average harmful-response score on unseen families
**Status:** ICML 2026 accepted paper; controlled seen-vs-unseen steering study on Mistral-v2-7B.

Primary source:
- Chen et al., *Steering Beyond the Support: Adversarial Training on Unsupervised Jailbroken Activation Simulation*, arXiv:2605.24535v2 / ICML 2026.

Evidence:
- The paper trains an AlphaSteer-style supervised safety-steering defense on limited subsets of jailbreak families and evaluates the same model on seen and held-out families.
- Across the three partial-support rows in Table 1, the reported **Avg (Unseen) StrongReject score is 32.29%, 29.23%, and 29.58%**, while some seen-family entries in those same rows are as low as **1.52–8.78%**.
- When all six attack families are included in training, the row has no unseen-family average and all six listed scores fall to **1.84–8.22%**.
- Positive control: the paper's own unsupervised bi-level latent-adversarial training is specifically designed to expand activation-space support and reports mostly sub-5% attack scores across the evaluated families.

Failure implication: a steering defense fitted to a static attack support can substantially overstate robustness if evaluation reuses those attack families. Safety steering should be evaluated on held-out attack families or an independently expanded latent support rather than only on the supervised defense set.

Scope guard: the 29–32% figures are held-out averages from the paper's particular partial-support splits, not a universal OOD error rate. The full-support row is not a matched causal comparison against exactly the same unseen distribution.

### FE-121 — Different automated jailbreak judges assign sharply different success rates to the same human-labeled responses because their error profiles are opposite
**Status:** June-2026 arXiv v2 preprint with released audit data/code; human-anchored judge calibration study.

Primary source:
- Gao, *How Reliable Is Your Jailbreak Judge? Calibration and Adversarial Robustness of Automated ASR Scoring*, arXiv:2606.25487v2.

Evidence:
- On **596 human-labeled HarmBench validation completions**, the dedicated classifier has **precision 0.835, recall 0.974**.
- Three LLM-as-judges have high precision (**0.81–0.94**) but highly variable recall (**0.06–0.65**).
- Because all judges score the same underlying response set, this directly implies that reported ASR can shift materially solely from judge choice, even before any attack on the judge itself.

Failure implication: an ASR number is not an evaluator-independent property of an attacker/defender pair. Judge precision/recall on a human-labeled slice is part of the measurement contract and should be reported alongside ASR.

Scope guard: the calibration set is HarmBench-derived and does not establish the same error rates on every domain, language, or model-output distribution.

### FE-122 — Content-preserving benign framing can flip 57–100% of LLM-judge harmfulness verdicts without removing the harmful content
**Status:** same June-2026 judge-audit preprint; adversarial evaluation of automated judges with human audit.

Primary source:
- Gao, arXiv:2606.25487v2.

Evidence:
- Wrappers that leave the harmful completion intact and add only benign framing flip the tested LLM-judge verdicts **57–100%** of the time.
- A single prepended refusal sentence accounts for a large share of the effect, with flips of **39–88%** depending on the LLM judge.
- Positive control: the dedicated HarmBench-style classifier changes on at most **6.7%** under these same surface attacks.
- A two-annotator audit of **80 sampled flips** found that **all 80 still contained the harmful content**, so the measured change is evaluator failure rather than successful harm removal.

Failure implication: a model can manipulate or accidentally bias an LLM-based safety judge through stylistic/refusal framing while leaving substantive harmful content unchanged. Output-format robustness tests are therefore necessary when an LLM judge is used to score jailbreak success.

Scope guard: this result applies to the tested judges and wrappers. It does not show that every LLM judge is equally fragile or that dedicated classifiers are robust to stronger white-box attacks.

### FE-123 — A dedicated safety classifier that resists surface wrappers can still fail under a small-budget white-box attack on the evaluator itself
**Status:** same June-2026 judge-audit preprint; white-box attack on confident true-positive evaluator cases.

Primary source:
- Gao, arXiv:2606.25487v2.

Evidence:
- Although the dedicated classifier is much more stable than LLM judges to content-preserving wrappers, a white-box GCG attack on its open weights flips **21 of 30 confident true positives = 70%**, with reported **95% CI 54–86%**.
- The attack plateaus within a small optimization budget; the paper reports a mean of roughly 21 optimization steps among successful cases and a 50-step cap.

Failure implication: passing shallow evaluator-robustness checks does not establish adversarial robustness of the metric. A judge can require different threat-model tests for black-box surface manipulation and white-box optimization.

Scope guard: the attack uses white-box access to an open classifier and only 30 confident true positives; this should not be extrapolated to proprietary judges or all evaluator architectures.
