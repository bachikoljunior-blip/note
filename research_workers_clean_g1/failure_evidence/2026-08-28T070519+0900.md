## New failure evidence

### FE-308 — A reasoning-based guardrail can itself become an availability attack surface; naive timeout policy creates a safety–availability dilemma
**Primary source:** Yuguang Zhou et al., “From Shield to Target: Denial-of-Service Attacks on LLM-Based Agent Guardrails,” arXiv:2606.14517, 12 Jun 2026, https://arxiv.org/abs/2606.14517

The paper optimizes natural-language payloads against one open-source surrogate guardrail and reports transfer across eight guardrail backbones, including proprietary models, with **13–63× token amplification**. In end-to-end code, web, desktop and multi-agent deployments, the same failure family causes up to **148× latency amplification**. The mechanism attacks the guardrail’s structured reasoning/schema-following behavior rather than merely making the protected agent produce an unsafe answer.

This produces an operational failure that ordinary safety metrics can miss. If a guardrail has a hard reasoning/token timeout, fail-closed handling preserves the denial of service, while fail-open handling can convert budget exhaustion into a safety bypass. A separate classifier tested by the authors assigns optimized payloads prompt-injection probability below 0.001, so a conventional injection prefilter is not sufficient in the tested setting.

**Failure implication:** guardrail evaluation should include adversarial compute/latency amplification and resource exhaustion, not only unsafe-action ASR. Cost bounds need an explicit terminal contract that does not silently become either “block forever” or “allow without a verdict.” Shared guardrail services should also be stress-tested for head-of-line blocking and cross-agent resource starvation.

**Scope guard:** the paper’s reasoning-style guardrails, payload optimization procedures, eight tested backbones, and four agent deployment settings. This is evidence of availability brittleness and timeout-policy tradeoffs, not evidence that every LLM guardrail is DoS-vulnerable or that the payloads necessarily produce unsafe downstream actions.

### FE-309 — After an incident, asking the same agent to infer its own remediation plan is materially weaker than structured recovery guidance, and some effects are not recoverable at all
**Primary source:** Zibo Xiao, Jun Sun, Junjie Chen, “AIR: Improving Agent Safety through Incident Response,” arXiv:2602.11749, 12 Feb 2026, https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.11749

AIR’s matched self-remediation ablation removes the rule-specified remediation component while keeping the incident context, available tools and current environment state. For the embodied-agent cases, remediation success falls from **92.308% to 80.952%** and response time rises from **22.191s to 28.682s**. For computer-use-agent cases, success falls from **97.727% to 76.596%** and response time rises from **25.943s to 34.591s**. The authors omit code-agent remediation from this metric because many code incidents involve **irreversible effects**, such as destructive file operations, that cannot be reliably remediated after the fact.

The same study reports that iteratively synthesized guardrail rules reduce incident counts across repeated sampled task rounds, which is a useful positive control: structured incident response can work. But the ablation shows that “the agent noticed a problem, so let it reason out a recovery” is not equivalent to having an explicit, validated containment/recovery contract.

**Failure implication:** recovery logic should be represented as externally inspectable procedure/state, with preconditions and an irreversible-effect boundary, rather than relying only on post-incident agent reasoning. A system should distinguish containment, reversible remediation, compensation, and effects that simply cannot be undone.

**Scope guard:** AIR’s three agent types and its sampled incident distributions. The percentages above are the paper’s embodied/CUA ablation; they do not establish a universal advantage for hand-written recovery rules, nor do they show that every LLM-generated rule is safe without validation.

### FE-310 — Per-agent checks can look clean until collaboration crosses a hidden evidence threshold; the harmful condition exists in accumulated cross-agent state, not any single message
**Primary source:** Jia-Hao Xiao, Lei Feng, Min-Ling Zhang, “When Collaboration Becomes a Trigger: Collective Evidence-Threshold Backdoors in Multi-Agent Systems,” arXiv:2608.01085, 2 Aug 2026, https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.01085

The paper constructs Boundary-Conditioned Backdoor Injection (BCBI), where separate poisoned agents contribute individually innocuous cues and the malicious behavior activates only after enough distinct peer evidence accumulates. In the main Qwen2.5-7B, threshold-3 setting, pre-threshold false-trigger rates are approximately **0–0.01**, while post-threshold attack success reaches roughly **0.95–1.00** across the six reported QA datasets. Correspondingly, post-threshold MAS accuracy collapses to approximately zero in the main no-defense/BCBI setting; examples include CSQA **0.76 pre → 0.00 post**, MMLU **0.72 → 0.00**, GSM8K **0.83 → 0.00**, ARCC **0.91 → 0.01**, OBQA **0.85 → 0.00**, and StrategyQA **0.70 → 0.00** in the reported table.

A clean-only latent-transition defense, LATTE, is a positive control: in the same main setting it substantially restores post-threshold utility by quarantining anomalous updates before they propagate. However, the paper does not establish robust performance against a fully adaptive attacker that explicitly optimizes around LATTE, and transfer results vary across backbones.

**Failure implication:** local moderation of each agent/message can miss failures whose trigger is a *joint predicate over accumulated communication*. Multi-agent safety should therefore test cross-agent evidence accumulation and make pre-commit checks stateful over the collective interaction, not merely inspect each contribution in isolation.

**Scope guard:** the authors’ synthetic collective-backdoor construction, tested MAS topologies/backbones/datasets and thresholded cue mechanism. This is not evidence that ordinary benign multi-agent collaboration spontaneously forms such backdoors, nor that LATTE is adaptively robust outside the tested attacks.

### FE-311 — Finetuning is not a security isolation boundary when an attacker knows the public pretrained ancestor; attack directions can transfer into private descendants
**Primary source:** Yixin Tan, Zhe Yu, Jun Sakuma, “One Leak Away: How Pretrained Model Exposure Amplifies Jailbreak Risks in Finetuned LLMs,” arXiv:2512.14751, first posted 14 Dec 2025; current public version inspected in this run, https://arxiv.org/abs/2512.14751

The paper’s threat model gives the attacker white-box access to a public pretrained model but **no parameters or finetuning data from the target descendants**. PGP uses the pretrained model and attacker-created surrogate finetunes to identify transfer-relevant directions, then attacks held-out finetuned targets. On AdvBench, average target success rate across five finetunes per backbone is **69.6, 62.6, 75.8, 89.0, 71.0, 86.2, 67.2, and 85.6%** for Llama2-7B, Llama2-13B, Llama3-8B, DeepSeek-7B, Qwen-7B, Qwen2.5-7B, Gemma-7B and Baichuan2-7B respectively. Under the same pretrained-known threat model, the adapted GCG baseline is much lower on the same columns (**24.4, 9.4, 12.4, 17.2, 17.8, 30.0, 24.4, 39.6%**).

The paper also shows that several standard defenses reduce rather than eliminate the transfer attack; for example, PGP remains **54.6–66.8%** across four reported backbones under a Circuit Breaker baseline, whereas a targeted PGP-Guard lowers those four values to **18.2–31.2%**. This is a positive control that the inheritance channel can be targeted, but does not erase the underlying failure mode.

**Failure implication:** a private finetuned descendant should not be treated as cryptographically or behaviorally isolated from a released ancestor. Security evaluation should include ancestor-informed transfer attacks, especially when the base model and finetuning family are public or reproducible.

**Scope guard:** the paper’s public-pretrain/private-finetune threat model, tested model families, tasks and attack construction. The authors explicitly note that their 70B-scale evaluation relies on LoRA finetunes and that applicability to 70B+ **full finetuning remains unclear**; do not generalize these transfer rates to arbitrary closed models or heavily transformed descendants.
