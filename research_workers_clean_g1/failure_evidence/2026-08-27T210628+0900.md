## New failure evidence

### FE-260 — Checkpoint/resume APIs can violate exactly-once and consume-once semantics even when persistence appears durable
**Primary source:** Sajjad Khan, “Resume Means Resume: A Machine-Checked Conformance Contract for Checkpoint, Interrupt, and Resume Semantics in Workflow Persistence Layers,” arXiv:2608.03836 (v3, Aug 2026), https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.03836

A deterministic LLM-free harness probes five pinned agent-workflow frameworks against six persistence properties. LangGraph 1.2.9 re-executes durably recorded work after a real SIGKILL, so one API is exactly-once across graceful interrupts but only at-least-once across crashes; it also persists schema-invalid state silently and records a second resume value that is never consulted. CrewAI 1.15.2 re-executes completed effect-bearing methods against its written claim, and pydantic-graph 1.x cannot resume after a mid-node crash. Under concurrent delivery, consume-once holds sequentially but fails: k processes resuming one parked interrupt can fire the gated effect k times, with failure saturation 1.0 in 36/40 tested cells and cross-host reproduction.

A positive control matters: the paper’s REMIT reference sequencer plus shared-store read-path consumption gate repairs the tested fork/validity/concurrent-consume cells by claiming the resume before any node executes.

**Failure implication:** “checkpoint exists” and “framework says resumable” are not enough for durable automation. Externally visible side effects require independent effect ledgers/idempotency keys, crash-path tests, concurrent-resume tests, schema validation, and a machine-checkable resume contract.

**Scope guard:** results are pinned to the tested framework versions/APIs and deterministic fault harness. They do not imply all versions or all persistence paths violate the same properties.

### FE-261 — Adaptive prompt/program tuning creates a winner’s curse: the selected benchmark winner can be optimistic enough to change deployment conclusions
**Primary source + author artifact:** Xu et al., “Towards Reliable LLM Evaluation: Correcting the Winner’s Curse in Adaptive Benchmarking,” arXiv:2605.05973, https://arxiv.org/abs/2605.05973 ; author repository https://github.com/jefferyz001/siren

The study evaluates adaptive random-search and DSPy prompt/program tuning on MMLU-Pro Math/Law across 11 open-weight models and four token budgets. Once benchmark items are reused inside tuning, the ex-post winning artifact is a selected random variable, so its observed benchmark score need not estimate fresh-data performance of the tune-then-deploy procedure. Controlled experiments show winner-based reporting is systematically optimistic and can reverse the decision about whether tuning beat the default. The author artifact reports that SIREN’s selection-aware repeated-split estimator stays within about ±0.21 percentage points of its Monte-Carlo procedure target in the reported comparisons, whereas PromptEval variants exhibit absolute bias at least 0.74 pp; sparse PromptEval can be pessimistic by as much as −12.72 pp while dense PromptEval approaches the optimistic winner.

**Failure implication:** an optimizer that searches prompts/policies on the same evaluation pool and then reports the best observed score can manufacture apparent improvement through selection. Evaluation must target the complete tune-then-deploy procedure with a disjoint held-out pool and selection-aware uncertainty, not just the winning artifact.

**Scope guard:** the formal guarantees condition on a frozen post-search shortlist and stabilized/smooth selection. Fully open-ended search during evaluation is outside the paper’s guarantee, and the empirical task family is MMLU-Pro rather than arbitrary agent trajectories.

### FE-262 — A deployed API model can degrade materially on a fixed private prompt bank without a public changelog
**Primary peer-reviewed source:** Wiese, “Human-anchored longitudinal comparison of generative AI with a bias-calibrated LLM-as-judge,” PLOS ONE 21(2):e0339920, 2026-02-02, https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0339920

The preregistered study ran the same private, hashed 240-prompt bank weekly for 10 weeks across three model families, logging exact API identifiers/parameters/timestamps and using blinded human correctness as the primary outcome. One model family remained stable, one improved, and Model C showed a statistically detected negative change point at Week 6; its largest observed Week-7 effect was Hedges’ g = −0.85 with 95% CI [−1.02, −0.68]. The Model×Week interaction was Holm-adjusted p<0.001. Weekly calibration also moved LLM-judge agreement with humans from roughly Kendall τ 0.38–0.52 uncalibrated to 0.59–0.68 calibrated.

**Failure implication:** a stable model name/config is not a stable behavioral dependency when using hosted services. Long-lived automations need dated canaries/change-point monitoring and should bind evidence to model identifier, request time and parameters; a prior repair or benchmark score can become stale without an explicit version change.

**Scope guard:** this is observational service drift over three model families and ten weeks; provider-side causes are unknown, so the change cannot be attributed to training, routing, alignment or architecture.

### FE-263 — A judge can favor its own/model-family outputs even on programmatically verifiable rubrics
**Primary source:** Pombal, Rei & Martins, “Self-Preference Bias in Rubric-Based Evaluation of Large Language Models,” arXiv:2604.06996v3, https://arxiv.org/abs/2604.06996

Using IFEval and LiveCodeBench with objective/programmatically verifiable rubrics, the paper measures false-positive rubric judgments only where the generator objectively failed. Judges can be more than 50% more likely to incorrectly mark a failed rubric as satisfied when evaluating their own output/model family. On HealthBench, self-preference shifts model scores by up to 10 points. Diverse judge ensembles reduce but do not eliminate the bias.

**Failure implication:** recursive self-improvement that uses the same model family to generate candidates and judge them can preferentially reward family-style outputs without genuine task improvement. Verifiable checks should dominate where available; otherwise use cross-family/human anchors and explicitly measure judge-generator interactions.

**Scope guard:** bias magnitude is benchmark-, rubric- and judge-dependent. The result does not show every self-judgment is biased or that ensembles are ineffective; it shows self/family identity remains a measurable confound even under objective rubrics.

### FE-264 — Plans can remain context-resident rather than becoming durable internal state; naive plan eviction causes large behavioral loss
**Primary source:** Mehta & Datta, “Plans Don’t Persist: Why Context Management Is Load Bearing for LLM Agents,” arXiv:2606.22953, https://arxiv.org/abs/2606.22953

Replay pairing compares matched trajectories with versus without the explicit plan in history. On Llama-3.1-70B, the measured plan signal is 0.453 one step after insertion and falls 4.1× in one action-observation cycle; HotpotQA falls 12.4×. A compression stress test reports that naive plan eviction cuts ALFWorld success by 34.7 percentage points, and probe-gated plan resurfacing does not recover the loss. Reasoning traces are themselves a measurement confound: strict removal of prior `<think>` blocks changes the estimated plan signal substantially.

**Failure implication:** a long-running automation cannot assume that an early instruction/plan has been “internalized” merely because it influenced recent reasoning. Compression/summarization/eviction should preserve action-critical plans and constraints in explicit durable state, and recovery tests should verify behavior after context turnover rather than only checking that a summary exists.

**Scope guard:** hidden-state cosine signal is a diagnostic, not proof of semantic memory content. Exact decay and the 34.7pp behavioral loss are tied to the tested models/tasks/compression regime; plan protection alone also did not solve all compressed-context failure.
