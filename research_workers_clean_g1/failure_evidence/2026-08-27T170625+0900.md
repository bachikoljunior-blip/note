## New failure evidence

### FE-211 — More inference-time interaction budget is not a substitute for diagnosing recoverable tool-environment failures
**Primary source:**
- https://arxiv.org/abs/2606.25819

`Beyond Function Calling: Benchmarking Tool-Using Agents under Tool-Environment Unreliability` evaluates a matched 200-task diagnostic subset under recoverable injected exceptions. The same perturbed tasks are compared under four conditions: baseline exception injection; test-time scaling (TTS) with 10 extra interaction rounds but no diagnosis; an oracle-style hazard diagnosis hint after failure; and a clean-environment oracle upper bound. Claude-Sonnet-4.6 scores `42.0 / 53.5 / 77.5 / 94.5%`; DeepSeek-V4-Flash `40.0 / 48.5 / 71.0 / 87.0%`; Doubao-Seed-2.0-Mini `45.0 / 53.5 / 70.5 / 85.5%`; Gemini-3.1-Flash-Lite `46.0 / 55.0 / 73.5 / 80.5%`; GPT-5.4-Mini `48.5 / 52.0 / 84.0 / 96.5%`. Thus extra interaction budget adds only `+3.5` to `+11.5pp`, whereas giving the correct failure diagnosis adds `+25.5` to `+35.5pp` over baseline and beats TTS by roughly `24–32pp` in these cells. Immediately after failed tool calls, retrying the same tool is the dominant local reaction in roughly `44–76%` of cases, yet the paper reports that this retry propensity is only weakly associated with end-to-end accuracy.

**Failure implication:** a tool agent can have substantial latent recovery capability while failing to identify the hazard class. Simply granting more steps or counting retries as resilience can therefore misdiagnose the bottleneck; anomaly identification and recovery policy must be tested separately from clean function-call skill and raw inference budget.

**Scope guard:** the intervention subset uses deterministic synthetic Python tools and oracle diagnosis text. This does not prove self-generated diagnosis would recover the same fraction, nor that test-time scaling is generally ineffective.

### FE-212 — Near-perfect clean tool success can coexist with severe observation-recovery failure on still-human-recoverable tasks
**Primary source:**
- https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.23635

`ToolRobustBench: Stage-Wise Perturbation Evaluation and Failure Diagnosis for Tool-Calling Agents` evaluates 15,456 single-family perturbation instances across seven models, 16 sampled local tools, four perturbation families, and 14 subtypes. Average clean success is `0.979`, yet family-average success falls to `0.918` for tool-interface perturbations, `0.773` for user-intent perturbations, `0.688` for runtime/environment perturbations, and only `0.455` for tool-output/observation perturbations; no evaluated model reaches `0.60` in the observation family. Severity averages fall from clean `0.979` to `0.869 / 0.724 / 0.491` under light/medium/heavy perturbation.

For the hardest return-evidence-loss subtype, human annotators judged the corrupted tool return still recoverable as YES or PARTIAL in `41/60 = 68.3%` of audited cases (`100%` light, `70%` medium, `35%` heavy), while the strongest model's measured success on that subtype is only `27.8%`.

**Failure implication:** high clean tool-calling accuracy is not evidence of robust use of incomplete/corrupted observations. A benchmark that tests only tool selection and clean schema compliance can miss a separate grounding/recovery deficit even where the answer remains recoverable from the returned evidence.

**Scope guard:** ToolRobustBench uses deterministic single-call local tools and controlled perturbations. The human-recoverability audit is 60 cases and should not be generalized to arbitrary production tool failures or long-horizon workflows.

### FE-213 — Combined tool-environment faults can be materially worse than either isolated constituent, so minimum-single-fault scoring is not a safe composition rule
**Primary source:**
- https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.23635

ToolRobustBench additionally evaluates five interpretable mixed-family subtype pairs. At medium severity, mean success under the mixed perturbation is `0.448`, versus `0.621` for the weaker isolated constituent and `0.754` for the mean of both constituents. At heavy severity, mixed success is `0.200`, versus `0.292` for the weaker constituent and `0.507` for their mean. Relative to the mean of the two isolated components, the mixed-family gap is approximately `-0.306` at medium severity and `-0.307` at heavy severity.

**Failure implication:** composing two independently measured reliability hazards by taking the worse isolated score can still substantially overestimate the joint condition. Reliability evaluation should include interaction tests for plausible co-occurring fault families instead of assuming separability.

**Scope guard:** the result covers five selected pairings in this benchmark; not every perturbation pair is negatively synergistic, and the paper explicitly excludes degenerate/incompatible mixtures. It does not establish a universal multiplicative or additive composition law.

### FE-214 — The visible failure symptom can be downstream of an earlier stage error; stage-targeted repair works for single/cascade faults but not multi-fault cases
**Primary source:**
- https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.23635

ToolRobustBench's causal diagnostics attribute `89.1%` of tool-interface failures and `89.9%` of user-intent failures to tool misselection, `91.7%` of tool-output/observation failures to incorrect arguments, and `97.6%` of runtime-family failures to runtime failure. A counterfactual repair audit fixes only the attributed primary error stage and re-runs the final task: Tier-1 single-primary-error cases recover `48/48 (100%)`, Tier-2 cascade cases recover `23/28 (82.1%)`, but Tier-3 multi-fault cases recover `0/22`; total recovery is `71/98 (72.4%)`. A targeted intervention on gpt-5.4-mini is also discriminative: adding chain-of-thought instructions to a B-family case whose diagnosed root is S1 tool selection leaves success unchanged at `21/30`, while explicitly clarifying perturbed tool-name aliases raises an A-family set from `21/30` to `30/30`.

**Failure implication:** fixing the last visible error or adding generic reasoning instructions can miss the true causal stage. Conversely, a single-root-cause diagnosis is insufficient once failures become genuinely multi-fault. Automation recovery should distinguish primary stage, cascade, and multi-fault structure rather than treating all failed traces as homogeneous.

**Scope guard:** the causal labels and repairs are benchmark-specific interventions, including a targeted gpt-5.4-mini study. The 0/22 multi-fault recovery result means the tested single-stage repair was insufficient, not that multi-fault failures are irrecoverable.

### FE-215 — High judge repeatability can coexist with low validity; exact-match agreement materially overstates chance-corrected agreement and benchmark rankings do not transfer cleanly
**Primary source:**
- https://arxiv.org/abs/2606.19544

`Reliability without Validity: A Systematic, Large-Scale Evaluation of LLM-as-a-Judge Models Across Agreement, Consistency, and Bias` evaluates 21 judge models from nine providers over MT-Bench, JudgeBench, and RewardBench, with 118 runs and roughly 541,000 judgments. On MT-Bench, exact-match agreement exceeds Cohen's kappa by `33.8–41.2pp` across all 21 judges. The paper also reports that judge rankings shift by as many as `14` positions across benchmarks. High test-retest reliability (`>=0.95`) can coexist with measurable position bias above `0.10` for some judges, and JudgeBench produces a `60.4pp` kappa spread versus `13.5pp` on MT-Bench, about `4.5×` greater discrimination.

**Failure implication:** a stable LLM judge can be consistently wrong or systematically biased, and raw percent agreement can look high because it ignores chance agreement and class structure. Promoting evaluator quality from one benchmark or from repeatability alone can therefore lock a control loop onto a biased objective.

**Scope guard:** the reported gaps are for the paper's pairwise judging tasks and rubrics. They do not imply every judge has the same direction or magnitude of bias, and high reliability remains useful when paired with external validity evidence.

### FE-216 — Fixed prompts and nominal service identifiers do not guarantee longitudinal comparability; production model behavior can drift on a weeks-long timescale
**Primary source:**
- https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371%2Fjournal.pone.0339920

A preregistered PLOS One study tracks three service-model families for ten weekly waves with a fixed 240-prompt bank, blinded human ratings as primary ground truth, and logged API parameters/model identifiers. One model family shows a significant mid-study degradation with a detected change point around week 6; its across-week variance is `0.98` versus `0.11` for the most stable family, and the largest reported week-7 correctness effect is Hedges' `g≈-0.85` (95% CI approximately `[-1.02,-0.68]`). Its safety policy consistency moves from `0.91` at week 4 to `0.75` at week 7 and back to `0.88` at week 10, while unsafe-flag rate moves `1%→3%→1%`. Separately, calibrating the secondary LLM judge against weekly human anchors raises judge-human Kendall agreement from roughly `0.38–0.52` uncalibrated to `0.59–0.68` calibrated.

**Failure implication:** a frozen benchmark artifact is not a frozen system when the evaluated endpoint is a hosted service. Historical scores, safety thresholds, and monitor calibration can become stale even under fixed prompts and nominal identifiers; repeated human-anchored checks are needed to separate service drift from evaluator drift.

**Scope guard:** this is an observational ten-week study of three model families; proprietary provider changes are opaque, so the study cannot identify the causal mechanism of the drift. It does not imply all hosted model endpoints drift at this rate.
