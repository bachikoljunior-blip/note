## New claims

### FE-098 — Shared-parameter safety self-play can suppress the red-team pressure that is supposed to reveal failures
**Status:** primary May 2026 preprint with released checkpoints/results; matched base-model transfer and cross-play evidence, but architecture/training differ in more than one respect.

Primary source:
- Mazzone et al., *The Attacker in the Mirror: Breaking Self-Consistency in Safety via Anchored Bipolicy Self-Play*, arXiv:2605.08427: https://arxiv.org/abs/2605.08427

Evidence:
- The paper contrasts Self-RedTeam, where attacker and defender share/update the same model parameters, with Anchored Bipolicy Self-Play (ABS), which freezes the base and trains separate role-specific LoRA adapters.
- Against the **same base Qwen target**, Self-RedTeam attackers reach **18% ASR (3B)** and **9% ASR (7B)**, while corresponding ABS attackers reach **35%** and **24%** respectively: +17pp and +15pp adversarial success for the role-separated attackers.
- The paper also reports roughly **10pp higher ASR in cross-play** for ABS attackers versus Self-RedTeam attackers of equal model size.
- Their theoretical analysis identifies a self-consistency bias in shared-parameter min-max training: attacker updates move the same decision surface used by the defender, weakening genuinely adversarial exploration.

Failure implication: a safety self-play loop can look stable while its attacker becomes too coupled to the defender to expose worst-case prompts. Shared training state is therefore itself an evaluation/training confound, not merely an efficiency choice.

Scope guard: ABS and Self-RedTeam differ in update separation, LoRA/full-finetuning details, and frozen-base structure, so the ASR gap is not a single-factor randomized estimate of parameter sharing alone. It is evidence for the tested systems plus a supporting theoretical mechanism, not proof that every shared-parameter self-play design under-explores attacks.

### FE-099 — Safety fine-tuning can improve refusal metrics while degrading general reasoning at small scale
**Status:** same primary study; 300-sample reasoning evaluations with significance annotations.

Evidence:
- Qwen2.5-3B base → Self-RedTeam changes:
  - Common Sense **0.699 → 0.503** (-19.6pp).
  - BoolQ **0.753 → 0.690** (-6.3pp).
  - ARC-Challenge **0.752 → 0.668** (-8.4pp).
  - GSM8K **0.816 → 0.796** (-2.0pp).
- The paper marks the Common Sense and BoolQ Self-RedTeam results as statistically lower under its McNemar + Bonferroni procedure at p=0.05 and describes the small-model loss as catastrophic forgetting.
- Positive control against overgeneralization: at 14B, Self-RedTeam's reported average reasoning accuracy is **0.878±0.035** versus base **0.874±0.030**, so the degradation is not monotonic across scale.

Failure implication: a post-training run can look safer on refusal benchmarks while silently losing useful reasoning capability, especially at smaller scale. Safety evaluation must keep capability retention in the same acceptance gate.

Scope guard: these are the paper's tested Qwen2.5 sizes, training recipe, and benchmark subsets. The result does not establish that safety fine-tuning generally causes catastrophic forgetting, and the 14B result is an explicit counterexample within the same study.

### FE-100 — A longer safety-LoRA run can abruptly collapse after an apparently improving checkpoint
**Status:** same primary study; single reported unstable 7B training trajectory with exact checkpoint metrics.

Evidence:
- Qwen2.5-7B ABS LoRA rank-32 at step 50 is still functional: WG ASR **0.217**, WJB ASR **0.022**, HarmBench ASR **0.437**, StrongREJECT RTA **0.915**, benign XSTest compliance **0.904**.
- At step 100, the same reported run collapses: WG ASR **0.807**, WJB **0.808**, DAN **1.000**, HarmBench **1.000**, harmful-XSTest ASR **0.984**, StrongREJECT RTA **0.220**, benign-XSTest RTA **0.017**, and benign compliance **0.032**.
- The authors explicitly note that some LoRA runs become unstable and produce nonsense output, with a characteristic repeated `!` sequence; Table 7 labels this checkpoint as a collapse to near-random behavior across benchmarks.

Failure implication: monotonic "more safety training" is not a safe assumption. Checkpoint-level multi-axis validation and rollback are necessary because an apparently useful trajectory can cross into catastrophic degeneration between evaluation points.

Scope guard: this is one documented unstable configuration/run, not an estimated collapse probability for LoRA or ABS in general. Other ranks/sizes in the same paper do not show the same collapse.

### FE-101 — Poison-filtering mitigations can trade missed poisons for destructive or incomplete sanitation
**Status:** ICML 2026 Spotlight primary Rapid Poison paper; mitigation evaluation under static handcrafted poisoning templates.

Primary source:
- Huang et al., *Rapid Poison: Practical Poisoning Attacks Against the Rapid Response Framework*, arXiv:2606.16242: https://arxiv.org/abs/2606.16242

Evidence:
- A PromptArmor-style guard LLM evaluated on 300 clean PAIR references plus 18 poisoned references for each of seven templates has **3.8% FPR** on clean references and **10.3% aggregate FNR** on poisoned references.
- Harder templates are materially worse: **24.1% FNR** for the SWE-bench template and **18.5% FNR** for MCQ format.
- Among detected true positives, content removal is not clean: **35%** are over-removed to empty strings, while **56%** retain structural artifacts from the injection template.
- Positive control: replacing the proliferation model with prompt-injection-aware Meta SecAlign 70B reduces the paper's professional-law target-domain FPR from **98% to 0%** for the tested static template.

Failure implication: "detect then sanitize" can fail on both sides of the interface: poisons can pass through, while detected samples may be destroyed or retain injection structure. A mitigation therefore needs end-to-end post-sanitization usability and residual-poison tests, not detector FPR/FNR alone.

Scope guard: the attacks here are static handcrafted templates and the Meta SecAlign result is a strong positive control under that same limited threat model. The paper itself states that adaptive prompt injection remains unevaluated in this mitigation experiment.

### FE-102 — Head-to-head cross-play can mis-rank robustness; per-checkpoint fresh best responses expose exploitability more directly
**Status:** AAAI 2024 zero-sum MARL primary study; not an LLM-safety experiment, but it satisfies much of the inherited strict evaluation design in a mature adversarial-learning domain.

Primary source:
- Chen et al., *Accelerate Multi-Agent Reinforcement Learning in Zero-Sum Games with Subgame Curriculum Learning*, arXiv:2310.04796v3: https://arxiv.org/abs/2310.04796

Evidence:
- For MPE, the authors freeze SACL checkpoints at **0M, 5M, 10M, ... 40M** environment samples and train an approximate adversarial best response against **each fixed checkpoint for 200M samples**.
- They repeat the procedure for **three seeds**, requiring **9 checkpoints × 3 seeds = 27 separately trained best-response policies** for a single exploitability curve.
- They explicitly separate this from ordinary cross-play and note non-transitivity: in Google Research Football, FSP/PSRO attackers can have higher win rates than SACL against particular PSRO/NeuRD defenders even while SACL has lower approximate exploitability.
- Table 1 gives that mismatch quantitatively. Approximate exploitability (lower is better) for SACL vs FSP vs PSRO is **0.35 vs 0.83 vs 0.80** in pass-and-shoot, **0.60 vs 0.78 vs 0.83** in run-pass-and-shoot, and **0.45 vs 0.63 vs 0.85** in 3-v-1-with-keeper.

Failure implication: a tournament win matrix can reward counter-policies without measuring worst-case vulnerability. Fresh best-response training against frozen checkpoints is a stronger adversarial evaluation primitive when the game is non-transitive.

Scope guard: this is zero-sum MARL, not LLM jailbreak/prompt-injection safety. It resolves the **methodological existence** of frozen-checkpoint + equal-budget + repeated-seed best-response evaluation, but it does not yet supply the inherited target's full LLM-safety attacker×defender cross-evaluation matrix.

## Strict-frontier search outcome
- The inherited strict target is **partially resolved methodologically** by SACL: there is a primary adversarial-learning study that freezes multiple checkpoints, trains a separate fixed-budget best response to each, and repeats the procedure across seeds.
- The still-missing LLM/agent-safety version is stricter: at least two frozen safety defenders, independently initialized fresh attacker optimization against each under equal target-call/query/search budget, then full all-attacker × all-defender cross-evaluation with repeated seeds.
- ARLAS/MAGIC remain near-misses because their attacker checkpoints inherit a shared co-evolution history rather than being fresh best responses to each frozen defender.
- The new ABS result adds a different warning: shared-parameter self-play can itself reduce attacker pressure, so a best-response evaluator must also avoid attacker/defender parameter coupling.
