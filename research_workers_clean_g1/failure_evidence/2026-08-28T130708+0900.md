## New failure evidence

### FE-338 — Repeated type-blind context compaction can erase safety constraints superlinearly even when the compactor is explicitly told to preserve them
**Primary source:** “The Compaction Cliff in Long-Running AI Agent Memory,” arXiv:2608.22752v1 / CIKM 2026 author version, 24 Aug 2026, https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.22752

The study runs the production-style prompt “compress to N tokens, keep every safety rule and procedural command verbatim” over real agent configurations. On the 20-configuration sequential-compaction subset at 50% per round, Claude Code’s `/compact` prompt on Sonnet 4.6 preserves only **53%** of classifier-labeled safety constraints after one round and **10%** after five rounds. The same qualitative decay appears across the tested LLM compactors and structural baselines. The preservation scorer was checked against two human annotators with **κ=0.92** on Preserved/Lost labels.

A typed positive control shows the failure is not an unavoidable consequence of compression alone. TypeCompact pins classifier-labeled constraints/procedures and reaches **1.00 / 0.95 / 0.80** constraint recall at 50% / 25% / 10% compression and stabilizes at **0.96** after repeated 50% rounds. Downstream paired tests also favor typed retention over deployable type-blind compaction on SafetyMed, retail, and airline; however the retail behavioral comparison is not token-matched, a limitation the authors state explicitly.

**Failure implication:** a long-running agent cannot treat repeated general-purpose summarization as a faithful durable store for safety constraints merely because every individual compaction prompt says to preserve them. Constraint retention needs a protected type-aware lane and verification after each compaction round.

**Scope guard:** the sequential headline is 20 sampled public agent configurations and a specific production-style compaction prompt/model; it does not establish a 53→10% decay law for every runtime. TypeCompact’s guarantee depends on correctly classifying constraints and on their joint footprint fitting the budget.

### FE-339 — Whole-task skill memories can reduce later task success, while changing only the induction granularity can flip the same memory mechanism from harmful to helpful
**Primary source:** “Break It Down, Pass It On: Cross-Task Skill Transfer in LLM Agents,” arXiv:2608.20274v1, 20 Aug 2026, https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.20274

Across AppWorld, OfficeBench, and KramaBench with 11 models, each skill condition is compared against the same agent with memory disabled. Skills induced from entire task trajectories lower average task-level-agent success by **1.2pp** for text skills and **4.1pp** for code skills, with losses on every benchmark and up to **7.4pp**. Applying the same induction prompt at subtask granularity instead raises average success by **1.9pp** for text and **0.5pp** for code.

The strongest mechanism check replays the subtask-level library into the *same task-level agent* while freezing further induction. On Qwen3-235B-A22B and GPT-OSS-120B, subtask-derived skills beat the original whole-task skills on every benchmark by **9.9pp on average**, up to **17.2pp** on AppWorld. The original whole-task library drags that same agent below its no-memory baseline on every benchmark; the subtask library restores it to baseline on OfficeBench/KramaBench and lifts AppWorld by **12.4pp**.

**Failure implication:** accumulating “successful experience” as reusable skills is not monotonically beneficial. The abstraction boundary used when creating a memory can turn experience into distracting, over-specific context even when retrieval itself works.

**Scope guard:** three long-horizon benchmarks and the tested 11 models/prompts. The subtask architecture normally adds planner/summarizer roles, but the frozen-library replay is the cleaner evidence that the effect travels with skill granularity. It does not imply all whole-task memories are harmful.

### FE-340 — Exact authorization can eliminate tested unsafe effects while imposing large benign-utility and latency costs; safety must not be scored without the enforcement frontier
**Primary source:** “AID-Guard: Stateful Authorization for Delegated Agent Effects,” arXiv:2608.21159v1, 21 Aug 2026, https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.21159

In the natural AgentDojo model-in-the-loop slice, the strict exact single-use manifest changes benign utility from **41/48→20/48** for DeepSeek V4 Flash (**−43.8pp [−66.7,−20.8]**) and **42/48→25/48** for Qwen3 Max (**−35.4pp [−58.3,−14.6]**), while protected unsafe effects fall to **0/144** for both models (unprotected: 1/144 and 7/144). The authors explicitly call exact-manifest a stress profile rather than a recommended default.

The prototype also has substantial implementation overhead: matched warm successful-path median latency is **6849.5 ms** protected versus **2918.7 ms** ambient, a **2.35×** median. A less rigid typed three-call frontier later raises observed benign completions from **12/48→22/48** for DeepSeek and **17/48→26/48** for Qwen while all **864** attack episodes in that frontier remain safe; because those endpoint calls expose no seed, the authors correctly do not claim a causal effect size for that comparison. Under direct complete-proposer compromise, the strong positive control blocks **44/44** attacks and admits **44/44** matched legitimate proposals.

**Failure implication:** a control system that minimizes unsafe-effect count alone can select an enforcement profile that is operationally over-restrictive or too slow. Optimizer acceptance should include benign completion, latency/resource overhead, and a typed/least-restrictive frontier rather than rewarding indiscriminate denial.

**Scope guard:** one academic Python/SQLite prototype, declared effect-path inventory, AgentDojo tasks, synthetic/controlled provider contracts, and bounded Stripe/Resend schedules. The natural slice has low attack exposure, and the no-seed frontier is descriptive rather than a randomized causal comparison.

### FE-341 — LLM-generated security-test harnesses can manufacture convincing crashes in stubs while confirming zero target weaknesses
**Primary source:** “LLM-Assisted Dynamic Threat Analysis for Attacker-Reachable Software Weaknesses in Autonomous Vehicles,” arXiv:2608.13450v1, 13 Aug 2026, https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.13450

The study derives 740 attacker-reachable Autoware sites and has two local open-weight LLMs, a no-static-context ablation, and a naive-template baseline generate **3,700** artifact sets, then compiles, repairs, and fuzzes executable harnesses against the real build. About **80% of first-shot compilation failures** are attributed to dependency wiring rather than program logic. The reasoning model compiles **64%** of harnesses first-shot versus **6%** for the code-specialized model, but complete object compilation after repair requires extensive stubbing; fewer than half of the reasoning model’s harnesses reach the fuzzer.

Most importantly, **all 37 observed crashes originate in stubbed code rather than Autoware**, and **no candidate weakness is dynamically confirmed within budget**.

**Failure implication:** “the generated harness compiled/fuzzed/crashed” is not evidence of a real target vulnerability unless the crash is provenance-traced back into the target system. Repair-through-stubbing can improve pipeline completion while simultaneously severing the semantic link to the artifact being audited.

**Scope guard:** one large autonomous-driving stack, sampled reachable sites, two local LLMs plus stated baselines, and the paper’s finite repair/fuzz budget. The result does not establish that LLM-assisted dynamic analysis cannot work on smaller or differently structured systems.

### FE-342 — In multi-agent deep research, the final orchestrator can introduce most report-level faithfulness/citation errors after upstream research has already occurred
**Primary source:** “Who is the Agent to Blame? Localizing Faithfulness and Citation Mistakes in Agentic Deep Research,” arXiv:2608.24306v1, 25 Aug 2026, https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.24306

The authors locally evaluate each agent invocation against its own inputs across three top-ranked open-source deep-research systems and classify hallucination, uncited-input reliance, uncited output, and insufficient citations. In AI-Q, **84.7% of final-report errors are attributed to the orchestrator**, with roughly **31%** of those orchestrator-origin errors described as hallucinations and the remainder citation mistakes. Agents that summarize a single document are the main exception to the paper’s broader finding that almost every role makes substantial mistakes.

Two targeted interventions derived from the localization improve citation recall by **5% without degrading output quality**, providing a positive control that the diagnosed synthesis-stage failure is operationally actionable rather than merely descriptive.

**Failure implication:** adding more upstream search/retrieval agents does not guarantee final-report faithfulness if the final synthesizer can drop citation links or hallucinate during aggregation. Multi-agent evaluation should attribute errors locally instead of scoring only the final artifact and assuming upstream retrieval quality is the bottleneck.

**Scope guard:** three evaluated open-source deep-research systems; the 84.7% figure is specific to AI-Q. The reported agent-to-error associations should not be generalized to every deep-research architecture.
