## New failure evidence

### FE-361 — Coding-agent capability estimates can move sharply with the harness under context pressure even when model weights, tasks, tools, serving, and evaluator are held fixed
**Primary source:** “Same Model, Different Harness: Different Coding-Agent Results,” arXiv:2608.26218v1, 26 Aug 2026, https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.26218

The study pairs a full chronological-conversation control with a treatment that keeps the same durable task record but mechanically shortens older tool results as context fills and adds fixed stall-loop/command-guard rules. Within a pair it holds task, model weights, context capacity, tool interface, serving setup, evaluator, harness code, and run/scoring limits fixed. On 169 SWE-bench Verified tasks with a 20,480-token window and fixed 480-second endpoint, mean per-task first-to-pass fraction rises from 28% to 49% and complete solutions from 43 to 72. With the same frozen treatment package on the same task cohort, first-to-pass also rises for Devstral (17%→37%), Nemotron (12%→18%), and Qwen3.8 (20%→35%). When context is expanded to 262,144 tokens so fewer than 1% of tasks hit the limit in every arm/benchmark, the Verified treatment difference largely disappears: -0.3 percentage points with 95% CI [-4.5,+3.9].

**Failure implication:** a coding-agent leaderboard score under a tight context window can be materially a property of the harness/context policy rather than a stable model-only capability estimate. Evaluations and optimizer acceptance should therefore pin the complete harness/context-management tuple and include an unconstrained-context or reading-boundary control when interpreting model changes.

**Scope guard:** the treatment is a bundled configuration, so this experiment does not identify the isolated causal effect of shortening, loop intervention, or command guards. The operating point was developed on the same benchmark families rather than a task-disjoint hyperparameter-development set, and the paper notes some run-era package-identity differences on Pro/FeatureBench. The near-zero wide-context contrast is specific to the tested models/tasks/endpoints.

### FE-362 — Recovering a valid terminal flag can substantially overstate demonstrated exploitation; ordered trace provenance changes both scores and model rankings
**Primary source:** “How Do LLM Agents Actually Get the Flag? Trace-Level Provenance for Agentic Offensive Security Evaluation,” arXiv:2608.26237v1, 26 Aug 2026, https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.26237

Across 1,435 CTF attempts from six models on 240 challenges, the authors reconstruct 1,056 recovered flags with two independent provenance judges. Of those recovered flags, 72.9% are backed by executed attacks, 10.9% are human-verified derivations without observable execution, and 16.2% are unsupported pathways. Benchmark-level genuine executed-exploit shares range from 62% to 86%. Requiring execution-backed evidence lowers every tested model’s score by 17.4–22.6%; pooled rank correlation with terminal-flag scoring falls to 0.60, while benchmark-level rank correlations range from -0.06 to 0.88. A direct command-level detector has F1 0.28, and run-summary features have AUC 0.491–0.586, so shallow action or summary inspection does not recover the missing provenance reliably in this corpus.

**Failure implication:** a correct terminal artifact is not necessarily evidence that the agent executed the capability the benchmark intends to measure. Agentic evaluations should bind credit to an ordered action→observation→state provenance chain rather than endpoint possession alone, especially when the endpoint can be exposed, recalled, or otherwise obtained without the intended mechanism.

**Scope guard:** this is an offensive-security CTF evaluation audit, not a general estimate for all agent benchmarks. Some challenge validity and environmental ambiguity interact with provenance classification, and the study explicitly distinguishes human-verified derivation from observable execution rather than treating all non-execution cases as malicious benchmark leakage.

### FE-363 — An executable authorization guard can still be weaker than a prompt policy when it trusts mutable local permissions; provenance-aware enforcement closes the tested laundering path
**Primary source:** “Multi-Agent AI Safety as an Institutional Design Problem,” arXiv:2608.09828v1, 10 Aug 2026, https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.09828

In a frozen 5,280-episode suite, the main delegation comparison has 384 episodes per governance condition. A concise prompt yields 27/384 realized violations, a local executable guard 22/384, a constitutional prompt 0/384, and a provenance-aware executable guard 0/384. All 22 local-guard violations occur in transformation-laundering scenarios. On the 96 matched laundering episodes, the local guard permits 22 violations while the provenance guard permits 0 (exact paired p=4.77e-7). The local guard consults mutable visible policy state; the provenance guard binds authority to the origin plus legitimate authority-changing events. Activation is model-dependent: 18/24 of the local-guard laundering violations occur with DeepSeek V3.2, four with Mistral, and none with the tested Gemini Lite or GPT-4.1 Mini settings. The two 0/384 conditions also reach zero by different mechanisms: the constitutional prompt produces no prohibited attempts, whereas the provenance guard blocks 51 prohibited attempts and achieves safe recovery in 44/51.

**Failure implication:** “there is a code guard” is not itself evidence of stronger authorization. If the guard trusts a mutable representation that an upstream transformation can rewrite, executable enforcement can codify the wrong authority. Evaluation should separately record prohibited intent/attempt, guard intervention, safe recovery, and final violation, because identical final violation rates can hide materially different control mechanisms.

**Scope guard:** these are small synthetic delegation environments with at most six actions; the delegation recipient does not launch a subsequent LLM call, so this is a multi-role workflow rather than open-ended multi-agent dialogue. The relative performance of prompts versus guards is not generalized beyond the tested models and authority-laundering construction.
