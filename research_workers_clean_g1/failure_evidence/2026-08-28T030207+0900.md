## New failure evidence

### FE-289 — Signed write provenance can eliminate unsigned poisoning yet still leave authenticated poisoning; provenance and influence control are separate defenses
**Primary source:** Tarun Sharma, “SMSR: Certified Defence Against Runtime Memory Poisoning in Persistent LLM Agent Systems,” arXiv:2606.12703v1, 10 Jun 2026, https://arxiv.org/abs/2606.12703

Across 15 enterprise scenarios and 3,150 repeated trials, SMSR’s HMAC-SHA256 write provenance reduces all tested unsigned memory-poisoning variants from 93–100% attack success to 0%. But when the attacker is authenticated and can create one signed injection, provenance alone no longer supplies the claimed bound; the paper adds randomized memory ablation plus verdict-based majority voting, which holds attack success to 8.0% (95% CI [5.8, 10.9], n=450). In an end-to-end query-only attack where the live agent itself writes the poison rather than receiving a pre-seeded record, combined SMSR reduces success from 65.3% to 5.3% (n=150). Clean-query utility is reported as 90% for Component 1 and 85% for the combined system.

**Failure implication:** cryptographic authenticity of a memory write answers “who/what path wrote this?” but not “how much influence may an authenticated but adversarial writer exert later?” A durable memory system therefore needs both origin authentication and a query-time influence bound or action-authority boundary. Treating “signed” as synonymous with “safe to act on” is unsound in the tested authenticated-adversary case.

**Scope guard:** this is an arXiv v1 single-author study over 15 enterprise-style scenarios. The 0% result applies to unsigned variants under the HMAC trust assumption; the 8.0% and 5.3% results apply to the paper’s specific smoothing/voting construction and attack budgets, not to arbitrary authenticated insiders or arbitrary persistent-memory architectures.

### FE-290 — Derivation lineage itself can be laundered; signing the agent’s summary or trusting a “trusted-tool echo” can certify the laundering step instead of the original authority
**Primary source:** Yedidel Louck, “Securing LLM-Agent Long-Term Memory Against Poisoning: Non-Malleable, Origin-Bound Authority with Machine-Checked Guarantees,” arXiv:2606.24322v1, 23 Jun 2026, https://arxiv.org/abs/2606.24322

The paper constructs three laundering channels—agent summarization, trusted-tool echo, and manufactured corroboration—that make untrusted content appear benign and can break or flip a derivation edge to “trusted.” Its formal result states that content- or lineage-based authority is not sound under those laundering channels, that write-time origin binding is necessary in the formal model, and that a non-malleable origin-bound authority construction with Sybil-resistant corroboration-gated elevation is sufficient under the stated assumptions. In the reported cross-defense/cross-attack/cross-model benchmark over eight frontier models, existing defenses reach up to 68% laundering ASR, while TMA-NM reports 0% ASR for both direct and laundering attacks with full legitimate utility.

**Failure implication:** FE-288’s lineage lesson needs a stronger guard: a derivation graph is useful only if an untrusted origin cannot be rewritten into a trusted edge by the very LLM/tool pipeline whose output the graph records. “Correctly signed by the agent” and “derived through a trusted tool” are not sufficient authority signals unless trust is non-amplifying from the original source through summarization, echo, and corroboration.

**Scope guard:** this is an arXiv v1 formal/benchmark paper. The 0% TMA-NM result is within its benchmark, laundering channels, trust model, and machine-checked specification; it is not evidence that all real deployments can implement origin binding or Sybil-resistant corroboration without error. The up-to-68% figure is a maximum across the tested matrix, not a universal failure rate for every lineage system.

### FE-291 — Lossy memory consolidation can erase source authority while preserving an action trigger; content sanitization cannot reconstruct provenance that was discarded
**Primary source:** Jinghan Xu, Yiyong Xiao, Wanru Shao, Hankai Liu, Xinjin Li, “Memory Provenance Laundering in LLM Agents: A Non-Amplification Firewall for Persistent Memory,” arXiv:2607.29167v1, 31 Jul 2026, https://arxiv.org/abs/2607.29167

The paper studies memory consolidation that rewrites an external observation into apparent user history or workflow support while preserving an action trigger and erasing the low-trust source. In its schema-grounded evaluation with fixed risk policies, vulnerable consolidated memories reach up to 1.000 ASR. With platform-maintained provenance, confirmation, and risk labels kept intact, no evaluated unauthorized high-risk action passes the PPMF gate, while confirmed benign actions and targeted low-risk memory use remain executable.

**Failure implication:** once a lossy summarizer discards source authority, downstream prompt filters or semantic sanitizers cannot reliably infer it back from content. Provenance should therefore be platform-maintained metadata that survives consolidation and should bound action authority, rather than text that the LLM is free to rewrite. This also supplies a positive control for FE-287: rejecting all memory is unnecessary when action risk is matched against retained authority.

**Scope guard:** this is an arXiv v1, EMNLP-submitted schema-grounded evaluation with fixed risk policies. The 1.000 ASR is a maximum in the vulnerable evaluation; the no-unauthorized-high-risk-action result applies to evaluated PPMF scenarios under intact platform provenance/labels, not to a compromised host or adversarially corrupted policy store.

### FE-292 — Signed tool receipts do not make hallucination detection complete; the semantic claim-to-receipt matcher remains a failure surface
**Primary source:** Abhinaba Basu, “Tool Receipts, Not Zero-Knowledge Proofs: Practical Hallucination Detection for AI Agents,” arXiv:2603.10060v1, 9 Mar 2026, https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.10060

NabaOS generates HMAC-signed tool-execution receipts that the LLM cannot forge and then classifies/cross-references response claims against those receipts. On NyayaVerifyBench (1,800 scenarios, four languages, six injected hallucination types), it detects 94.2% of fabricated tool references, 87.6% of count misstatements, and 91.3% of false-absence claims; in deep delegation, independent re-fetching catches 78.4% of URL fabrications. The paper reports under 15 ms verification overhead for the receipt-based checker, versus near-perfect zkLLM coverage at roughly 180,000 ms/query in its comparison.

**Failure implication:** cryptographic receipts can make “this tool call record was forged after the fact” hard, but they do not by themselves decide whether a natural-language claim is entailed by the recorded result. The semantic matcher, aggregation logic, absence reasoning, and deep-delegation recheck remain fallible. Execution receipts should be treated as evidence primitives, not as a complete correctness or authorization proof.

**Scope guard:** the reported coverage values are from the paper’s synthetic/injected NyayaVerifyBench and its deep-delegation task set; they do not estimate production hallucination rates. HMAC receipts also assume key/runtime integrity and do not by themselves show that a tool call was authorized, only that the recorded execution evidence can be authenticated under the scheme.

### FE-293 — A denied action can leak information through its denial outcome; flat data provenance misses causal information flows created by policy feedback
**Primary source:** Mohammad Hossein Chinaei, “Causality Laundering: Denial-Feedback Leakage in Tool-Calling LLM Agents,” arXiv:2604.04035v1, 5 Apr 2026, https://arxiv.org/abs/2604.04035

The paper constructs a denial-feedback attack in which an adversary probes a protected action, learns from whether/how it is denied, and later exfiltrates the inferred information through an apparently benign tool call. The leakage is not a direct flow from protected data, so a flat provenance baseline misses it. The proposed Agentic Reference Monitor adds denied-action nodes and counterfactual causal edges to the provenance graph; in three controlled scenarios it blocks causality laundering, transitive taint propagation, and mixed-provenance field misuse that the flat baseline misses, with sub-millisecond policy-evaluation overhead.

**Failure implication:** a complete-mediation boundary must model information created by the enforcement mechanism itself. “The protected call was denied, therefore no information crossed the boundary” is false when the denial is observable and correlated with secret state. Provenance/receipt systems need denial-aware causal dependencies or deliberate non-informative denial behavior when denial outcomes reveal protected predicates.

**Scope guard:** this is an arXiv v1 controlled study with three representative scenarios and a formal/runtime prototype. It does not quantify production leakage prevalence or prove the proposed causal graph remains complete under arbitrary adaptive multi-step probing.
