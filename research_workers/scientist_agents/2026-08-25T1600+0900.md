## High-confidence findings

### SA-20260825-01 — Separate evidence retrieval/verification from hypothesis synthesis

**Mechanism.** Use specialized literature-retrieval agents to produce grounded reports, followed by a distinct deeper verification/synthesis layer rather than allowing a general LLM to invent references while reasoning.

**Primary evidence.** Ghareeb et al., *A multi-agent system for automating scientific discovery*, Nature 655, 497–505 (published 19 May 2026), https://doi.org/10.1038/s41586-026-10652-y . In Robin, Crow and Falcon are specialized literature agents. In an ablation, replacing Crow with o4-mini for 15 assay proposals produced **44.5 ± 6.37% hallucinated references**, whereas Crow produced **0 hallucinated references** in the human reference check. Ablating Falcon or both Crow and Falcon also increased hallucinated references, and 50-vs-50 proposal tournaments showed lower judged proposal quality after any Crow/Falcon ablation. The paper explicitly notes that Falcon can correct hallucinated references originating upstream.

**Transferable implication.** For scientific agents, retrieval and reference verification should be a separately testable subsystem with provenance-preserving outputs; letting the same generative model both reason and supply citations is an avoidable failure path.

**Scope / uncertainty.** The 44.5% figure is from a specific dAMD assay-proposal setting and n=15 proposals. Do not generalize it as a universal hallucination rate. Proposal quality was judged by an LLM tournament, not solely humans.

### SA-20260825-02 — Tool-using agent harnesses give large gains, but multi-step scientific analysis remains the bottleneck

**Primary evidence.** In the same Nature Robin paper, Finch was evaluated on a 170-question expert-curated BixBench subset. Finch scored **22.8 ± 1.7%** versus **1.6 ± 1.2%** for Claude Sonnet 3.7 alone (n=3), about a 14x absolute-ratio improvement from the agent harness. Yet performance split sharply by task structure: **47.9 ± 1.5%** on statistics versus **15.3 ± 2.0%** on bioinformatics, where tasks often required parameter-sensitive multi-step pipelines. On task-specific workflow prompts used inside Robin, Finch reached **86 ± 0%** rubric adherence on RNA-seq and **100 ± 0%** on flow cytometry (n=3 runs each).

**Citation-chain evidence.** Mitchener et al., *BixBench: a Comprehensive Benchmark for LLM-based Agents in Computational Biology*, arXiv:2503.00096, https://arxiv.org/abs/2503.00096 . The original BixBench reports nearly 300 open-answer questions over >50 scenarios and only **17% accuracy** for frontier-model agents in the open-answer regime.

**Transferable implication.** The useful unit is not “LLM intelligence” alone but an execution harness that can operate on files/data/tools with explicit task decomposition. However, long multi-step pipelines need additional recovery, state tracking, parameter validation, and intermediate verification; tool access alone does not solve them.

**Scope / uncertainty.** Finch task-specific scores use strong task prompts and the Robin environment, so they should not be interpreted as broad autonomous bioinformatics competence.

### SA-20260825-03 — Staged experiment management + agentic tree search is an empirically supported way to spend test-time compute

**Primary evidence.** Lu et al., *Towards end-to-end automation of AI research*, Nature 651, 914–919 (published 25 Mar 2026), https://doi.org/10.1038/s41586-026-10265-5 . The template-free AI Scientist organizes experimentation into four explicit stages: feasibility prototype, hyperparameter tuning, main research agenda, and ablation studies. Each stage runs its own tree search; an evaluator selects the best leaf to seed the next stage. The paper reports a strong positive relationship between allocated compute / tree-search depth and generated-paper quality. Across model releases, paper quality also improved significantly with underlying model release date (**P < 0.00001**). Its automated reviewer achieved **69% balanced accuracy** on pre-cutoff ICLR decisions and **66%** post-cutoff, comparable to the cited human 66% benchmark; five reviews are ensembled into a meta-review.

**Human external check.** Three fully AI-generated manuscripts were submitted (with venue cooperation) to an ICLR 2025 workshop. One received scores **6, 7, 6** (mean **6.33**) and ranked in the top 45% of reviewed papers; the authors state none met the higher main-ICLR bar.

**Transferable implication.** Instead of one long monolithic research loop, allocate compute by stage and prune between stages. Preserve explicit experiment journals and stop criteria. This creates clean checkpoints for viability, optimization, hypothesis testing, and ablation.

**Scope / uncertainty.** The compute-scaling figure is evaluated primarily via the paper’s own automated reviewer. Workshop acceptance is a low-N external check (3 submissions, 1 above threshold) and not evidence of main-conference-level autonomous research.

### SA-20260825-04 — Self-healing execution is more important than code generation once tasks become iterative

**Primary evidence (recent preprint, controlled backbone).** Liu et al., *AutoResearchClaw: Self-Reinforcing Autonomous Research with Human-AI Collaboration*, arXiv:2605.20025, https://arxiv.org/abs/2605.20025 . ARC-Bench uses 25 experiment-stage topics with the same GPT-5.3-codex backbone and sandbox/time budgets across systems. AutoResearchClaw Full-Auto scored **0.596 overall**, versus **0.511** for AIDE-ML and **0.419** for AI Scientist v2. Code-development scores were already high (0.938/0.958/0.712), but execution separated systems: **0.562** Full-Auto vs **0.415** AIDE-ML and **0.442** AI Scientist v2. The system diagnoses failed runs and chooses `Refine` (repair/retry) or `Pivot` (change direction while preserving information). Full-Auto failed to produce valid results on 2/25 topics versus 6/25 for AI Scientist v2, with failures concentrated in iterative-refinement tasks.

**Transferable implication.** Treat failed experiments as observations, not terminal errors. Persist partial artifacts/logs; classify failure into repairable execution defects vs evidence that the research direction should change; make that classification a first-class decision.

**Scope / uncertainty.** ARC-Bench and its rubric-assisted LLM judge are introduced by the same authors and the paper is a 2026 preprint, so this is promising but lower-confidence than the Nature / ICLR evidence above.

### SA-20260825-05 — Self-correction should be task-matched: guided checks for procedures, holistic reasoning checks for quantities

**Primary evidence.** Panapitiya et al., *AutoLabs: cognitive multi-agent systems with self-correction for autonomous chemical experimentation*, Scientific Reports 16, 19554 (published 25 Jun 2026), https://doi.org/10.1038/s41598-026-45593-z . Across **20 agent configurations** and five chemical protocol benchmarks, reasoning-enabled configurations reduced numerical chemical-amount error (nRMSE) by **>85%** on complex tasks. The best multi-agent + tool + guided-self-check configuration achieved **F1 > 0.89** on challenging multi-plate syntheses. Unguided self-checks reduced nRMSE by **37%** for a non-reasoning single-agent and **>50%** for all other compared configurations, but could *degrade procedural F1* by introducing extra steps. Guided checks consistently improved procedural correctness. The paper summarizes the division of labor: guided checks are better for procedure/F1; unguided holistic reasoning checks are better for numerical nRMSE.

**Transferable implication.** Do not use a single generic “reflect and improve” loop. Attach explicit verification modes to artifact type: checklist/rubric validation for discrete procedural constraints; holistic reasoning and recomputation for numerical consistency.

**Scope / uncertainty.** Chemical-protocol generation is a specific domain, but this is a systematic peer-reviewed architecture ablation and is strong evidence for task-conditioned verification design.

### SA-20260825-06 — Human help is most valuable at sparse high-leverage decision points, not uniformly at every step

**Primary evidence (recent preprint).** In AutoResearchClaw’s end-to-end HITL ablation (10 topics, 7 intervention regimes), Full-Auto produced mean paper quality **4.03** with **25.0%** accept rate; Gate-Only **5.03 / 50.0%** with 3 interventions; CoPilot **7.27 / 87.5%** with 6 interventions; Step-by-Step **5.19 / 50.0%** with 23 interventions; Thorough **4.86 / 42.9%** with 8 interventions. The authors’ qualitative case study attributes the CoPilot advantage to intervening around experimental semantics and claim checking rather than dense supervision.

**Corroborating peer-reviewed evidence.** AutoLabs reports that expert collaboration improved F1/nRMSE in most experiments, but the expert still missed granular procedural errors (e.g. stir rates, capping/plate assignments). The authors argue for expert oversight on strategy plus automated validation for details.

**Transferable implication.** Escalate to humans/stronger supervisors at research-direction choices, experiment semantics, and final claim-to-evidence checks; avoid requiring approval for every mechanical step. Dense intervention can add latency without preventing all low-level errors.

**Scope / uncertainty.** The strongest quantitative HITL comparison is from the AutoResearchClaw preprint with only 10 topics; treat exact rates as benchmark-local.

## Capability ceilings / negative evidence (scoped)

- Chen et al., *ScienceAgentBench*, ICLR 2025 / arXiv:2410.05080, https://arxiv.org/abs/2410.05080 : 102 real data-driven discovery tasks from 44 peer-reviewed papers. Best baseline agent solved **32.4%** independently and **34.3%** with expert-provided knowledge across three attempts; o1-preview reached **42.2%** with >10x the cost. This is evidence that inference-time compute helps but does not close long-horizon scientific execution gaps.
- Starace et al., *PaperBench*, arXiv:2504.01848 / OpenAI 2025, https://arxiv.org/abs/2504.01848 : 20 ICML 2024 Spotlight/Oral replication tasks, 8,316 gradable rubric items; best tested main agent (Claude 3.5 Sonnet New + scaffold) achieved **21.0% average replication score**, below top ML PhD baselines. This bounds end-to-end research replication capability in that setup.
- Huang et al., *MLAgentBench*, ICML 2024 / arXiv:2310.03302, https://arxiv.org/abs/2310.03302 : 13 ML experimentation tasks; best reported Claude 3 Opus agent averaged **37.5% success**, with 0% on some recent Kaggle tasks. The authors identify long-term planning and hallucination as key challenges.

Negative evidence is limited to the specific benchmark/model/scaffold configurations above; it is not a claim that autonomous science broadly cannot work.

## Citation-chain supporting evidence

- Skarlinski et al., *Language agents achieve superhuman synthesis of scientific knowledge* (PaperQA2), arXiv:2409.13740, https://arxiv.org/abs/2409.13740 . PaperQA2 matched/exceeded experts on literature tasks and identified **2.34 ± 1.99 contradictions per paper**, **70%** expert-validated. This supports specialized retrieval/synthesis agents but does not directly validate end-to-end autonomous discovery.
- Asai et al., *Synthesizing scientific literature with retrieval-augmented language models* (OpenScholar), Nature 2026, https://doi.org/10.1038/s41586-025-10072-4 . On ScholarQABench, OpenScholar-8B outperformed GPT-4o by **6.1%** and PaperQA2 by **5.5%** in correctness in the version of record; OpenScholar-GPT-4o improved GPT-4o correctness by **12%**; experts preferred OpenScholar-GPT-4o over expert-written answers **70%** of the time. This strengthens the case that evidence-substrate/retrieval design can dominate base-model size for literature synthesis.
